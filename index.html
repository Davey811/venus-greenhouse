The Venus Test

Venus's surface is at 735 K, hot enough to melt lead. Is that the 92 bar of pressure, or the greenhouse effect? Same planet, same sunlight, same pressure, same lapse rate. Two runs, and an energy ledger that has to balance.

The argument, step by step

  1. Venus is in energy balance. It absorbs about 150 W/m² of sunlight, averaged over the planet, and sends the same back to space as infrared. Measurements by the Pioneer Venus Orbiter confirmed that the planet is in overall energy balance to within the accuracy of the measurements. Taylor & Grinspoon 2009, §2.4
  2. Without infrared absorbers, the ground can't be warmer than 227 K. If the air doesn't absorb infrared, it doesn't emit any either: the ground is the only thing that radiates, and all of its radiation escapes. So σT⁴, averaged over the ground, equals the 150 W/m² absorbed, and the ground sits at 227 K whatever the pressure, the winds, the convection or the lapse rate. A ground with hot and cold patches averages lower, not higher. Even if Venus reflected nothing, the limit would be 327 K. An airless body with the same albedo and heliocentric distance as Venus would reach radiative equilibrium at a mean surface temperature of only about 230 K. §2.4
  3. The real ground is at 735 K, and its infrared doesn't get out. At 735 K the ground emits about 16,600 W/m² of infrared, 110 times what Venus absorbs and 25 times all the sunlight that reaches Venus. Only 150 W/m² leave Venus. The other ~16,400 W/m² of the ground's infrared is absorbed by the air. That is the greenhouse effect: the infrared radiation energy trapped by atmospheric gases and clouds. Raval & Ramanathan 1989 On Earth the same difference is about 156 W/m² (396 − 240); on Venus it is about 100 times larger. This step doesn't depend on how energy moves inside the atmosphere, only on how much leaves the ground and how much leaves Venus.
  4. The ground lives on what the air sends back. Enough sunlight diffuses through the cloud layers on Venus to provide about 17 W per m2 of average surface insolation. §2.4 A ground that emits 16,600 W/m² and gets 17 W/m² from the Sun receives at least ~16,560 W/m² from the air above. Without the infrared trapped in step 3, the air would only have the ~130 W/m² of sunlight it absorbs itself to pass on, a hundred times too little.
  5. The lapse rate fills in the shape. The absorbed energy must instead be raised by convection along an approximately adiabatic temperature-pressure profile to a level near the cloud tops where it can radiate to space. §2.4 On Venus that level is about 71 km, where the air is at 225 K; below it the temperature rises by about 7.1 K per km: 225.30 + 7.145 × 71.38 = 735.3 K. The absorbers set the height, the lapse rate sets the slope. This step explains the profile, it doesn't prove anything: the 71 km and the slope are read from the measured profile, so 735 K comes out by construction. The proof is in steps 1 to 4.

The First Law, in time

Add absorbers and the emission level moves up. For a while Venus radiates from colder air: less power leaves than arrives, and the difference is stored as heat, dU/dt = Pin − Pout. The column warms at the same lapse rate until the new level radiates the absorbed power again. Nothing is created: the warming is paid for by the imbalance.

Rough time scale: the whole atmosphere warms together at a fixed lapse rate, with heat capacity C = (p₀ / g) × cp = (9.21 × 10⁶ Pa / 8.87 m/s²) × 1.1 kJ/(kg·K) ≈ 1.1 × 10⁹ J/(m²·K), cp of CO₂ at 600–700 K (Çengel and Cimbala, Table A-10). The end state does not depend on C; only the timing does.

If compression paid the bill

Suppose there were no greenhouse effect and the ground were kept at 735 K by compression. The ground loses more than the sunlight that reaches it, every second. Compression can supply that only while the air is actually being compressed: gravity does work as the column sinks, at a rate p₀ × v per square metre.

Objections

"Taylor and Grinspoon themselves say it's the mass of the atmosphere."

They do: the high surface temperature on Venus, is largely a consequence of the large mass of the atmosphere, rather than any mysterious thermal process (§2.2). The same paper says how the mass works: Most of the energy deposited at depth cannot escape as long-wave radiation and has to be carried up to near the cloud tops before it can radiate to space (§2.4). A massive CO₂ atmosphere is a thick layer of infrared absorber, and that is what lifts the radiating level to 71 km. In the same paper, switching off CO₂'s absorption in the model cools the surface by 423 K (Table 2).

"They also say Earth with 92 bar would be hot even without more greenhouse gas."

The sentence is: If the Earth had such a high surface pressure, it too would be extremely hot, even without the increased proportion of greenhouse gases that is found on Venus (§2.2). With the same proportions, 92 times the air means 92 times the CO₂ above every square metre, and the higher pressure broadens the absorption lines: the absorber grows with the mass. What doesn't work is mass without absorbers, which is the first run: 227 K.

"It's just the adiabatic lapse rate."

The measured profile, in the paper's words, roughly follows the hydrostatic and adiabatic formulae (§2.2). The lapse rate says how much warmer the ground is than the level that radiates to space; it doesn't say where that level is. With no absorbers that level is the ground itself, and the ground sits at 227 K.

"Compression heats the gas."

Compressing a gas warms it while it is being compressed: the work done is pressure times the change in volume. Venus's atmosphere isn't being compressed. A pressure that doesn't change does no work, while the ground radiates away 16,600 joules per square metre every second. To pay that by compression the air would have to sink by about 57 km a year, and the whole gravitational energy of the atmosphere would last about three months: see "If compression paid the bill".

"Venus is closer to the Sun."

Venus reflects about 76% of the sunlight it receives and absorbs less than Earth: the paper gives equilibrium temperatures of about 230 K for Venus and 250 K for Earth (§2.4). Even if Venus reflected nothing, a ground that radiates freely to space couldn't average more than 327 K. Try it in the first run. And Mercury, closer and darker, absorbs 14 times more sunlight and is 300 K colder: see "The Mercury test".

"The heat comes from the interior, from volcanoes."

Interior heat release cannot, in any case, be the dominant influence in surface temperature, because of the constraints placed by the energy balance measurements (§2.4). What leaves Venus matches the sunlight it absorbs.

"The first run is unrealistic: no gas at 92 bar is transparent in the infrared."

Right, and that absorption is the greenhouse effect. The first run is the control: same mass, same sunlight, no infrared absorption. Whatever the real air adds on top of it, it adds by absorbing infrared.

"The air could give the heat back by convection, not by radiation."

It doesn't matter how the energy gets back to the ground. The ground sends out 16,600 W/m² as infrared and only 150 W/m² leave Venus, so the rest is absorbed by the air. Air that absorbs the ground's infrared is the greenhouse effect, whatever happens next. And on Venus convection carries energy up, not down: it is raised by convection towards the cloud tops (§2.4).

"You tuned the second run to give 735 K."

Yes: its height and slope are read from the measured profile, so it matches Venus by construction. It is there to show the shape, not to prove the greenhouse effect. The proof doesn't use it: it uses the ground's temperature, the energy that leaves Venus and the sunlight that reaches the ground, all measured.

"You need the adiabatic lapse rate to compute the emission height."

No. The emission height comes from radiation and hydrostatics alone: with the absorber mixed in the air, τ(z) = τ₀ p(z)/p₀, and Venus radiates from where τ ≈ 1, so ze = H ln τ₀, with H = RT/(Mg). The lapse rate is needed only to go from that level down to the ground. And with radiation alone, no convection, the ground would be far hotter: press "Show radiation only" under the chart. Convection, at the adiabatic lapse rate, is what brings it down to 735 K.

"At 1 bar Venus is at 15 °C, like the Earth."

Not in the measurements. In the Venus International Reference Atmosphere the pressure is 1 bar at 50.5 km, where the air is at 346 K, 73 °C. The air is at 15 °C higher up, at 56.8 km, where the pressure is 0.4 bar. Press "Show the 1-bar level" under the chart. Anchoring Earth's surface temperature at Venus's 1-bar level is a choice, not a measurement, and the anchor is the whole question.

"It's the Sun: the long Venus day bakes the surface."

The solar day on Venus lasts about 117 Earth days, so the nights last about two months. NASA lists Venus's day–night temperature range as ~0: the surface doesn't cool in the dark. Mercury, with a longer day and 14 times more absorbed sunlight but no atmosphere, averages 440 K. See "The Mercury test".

"Only 17 W/m² of sunlight reaches the ground. How can it be the greenhouse effect?"

That's the strongest evidence for it. 17 W/m² of sunlight can't keep up a ground that emits 16,600 W/m². Almost all of the energy the ground receives arrives as infrared from the air above.

Method. Sunlight absorbed: 2601.3 × (1 − albedo) / 4. Emission level: a sphere of radius R + h radiates the same power as Tₑ over the ground, so it sits at Tₑ × (R / (R + h))^½, with R = 6051.8 km: 225.3 K at 71.38 km, where the measured profile reaches that temperature. Lapse rate: (735.3 − 225.3) K / 71.38 km = 7.145 K/km, the mean of the measured profile from the ground to that level. The ground is treated as black in the infrared; rock is close to it (an emissivity of 0.9 would turn 227 K into 233 K). Greenhouse effect: infrared emitted by the ground minus infrared sent to space. "Must come back from the air" is what the ground emits minus the sunlight it absorbs; any heat the air's motions carry away from the ground only adds to it. The slider keeps today's 17 W/m² at the ground for every height. This is a test of energy bookkeeping, not a climate model: the full calculation is the published model.
